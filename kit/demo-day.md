# Demo day — the rubric and the retrospective

Takeaway kit · Intro to the Stock Market · written for lesson 12

Twelve weeks ago the course promised to grade decisions, not outcomes. Tonight it does so in public: every student presents one decision from their journal and the room judges the process — what the rule said, how the size was chosen, where the stop was, what was written down, what was done when it mattered — and says nothing about what the price did. This page is the rubric, the audience's one job, and the questions the course asks of itself at the end.

## 1 · Choose the decision

One ticket from your paper account — an open or a close — with the journal entry you wrote at the time and whatever you wrote after. The best choice is not the one that worked. It is the one you can account for: the rule's state the day you wrote it, the size the calculator gave and what you took, the stop and your plan for it. A decision that went wrong by the rules is a better exhibit than one that went right by luck, and the room will know the difference by the end of the evening.

Bring the review page's audit for your account (`/me` → "review my process"): its lines are the referee's. The audit reads the rows and says *Followed*, *Not followed*, or a note on the journal — it has no opinion about the price and neither will the room.

## 2 · The rubric — five questions, in this order

| | The question | What a full answer has | What it never has |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **What did the rule say?** | The state on the close you acted on (LONG since when, or FLAT), the two averages' order, the signal's own date | "I had a feeling", "everyone was buying" |
| 2 | **How many, and why?** | The calculator's number — the instrument risk, the account value, the target — and the units you took (fewer allowed, never more) | A round number chosen for comfort; "all in" |
| 3 | **Where was the stop, and what was the plan?** | The level (the high since entry less half the instrument risk — ½ × instrument risk × entry price, the rules card's rule), the fact that it trails, and the sentence "if it is hit I close on the next close" | A stop moved down; a plan to "see how it goes" |
| 4 | **What did you write down, and when?** | The entry before the decision — trigger, thesis, what would prove you wrong, the size and why, the review date — and the entry after | A journal written after the fact to fit the result |
| 5 | **Did you do what you wrote?** | The audit's line for this position; if the stop was hit, the close ticket within the week; if it was not, nothing done | "I was going to, but it bounced" |

Each question is answered in a sentence or two; the five together take four minutes. A sixth question — *did it go up?* — is not asked, and a presenter who answers it anyway is thanked and asked question 5 again.

## 3 · The audience's one job

One question each, on process, from the five above. The tutor's rule for the room: a question about the result is a question about luck, and the course has nothing to say about luck. The good questions sound like: *what was the instrument risk that night? · why 17 shares and not 18? · what would have proved you wrong? · when the stop was hit, what was the first thing you did?* The best question of the evening is the one that makes the presenter open their journal to check.

## 4 · What the room says at the end of each presentation

Not a mark. Three sentences, from three people: one thing the process did well; one thing the journal could have carried; one thing the room learned. The presenter writes all three in the journal under the entry. That is the grade — a record, not a score — and it is the same grade a nightly report gives: what happened, what the rules did, what to write down next time.

## 5 · The course's own retrospective

The course is judged by its own rubric. Five questions, asked of the twelve weeks, answered honestly in the room:

1. **What did the rules say?** The course chose Graham for *what* and Carver's Starter System for *when and how much*, and wrote both into a toolkit that enforces them. Were the rules clear enough to follow without the tutor in the room? Where did a rule need a human (the quarantine, the unknown split, the unlinked account)?
2. **How much, and why?** Paper money only, $10,000 a head, a 12% risk target. Did the size of the paper account make the lessons real? Did anyone want it bigger, and why?
3. **Where was the stop?** The course's stops were its checks: the explainer's and the tutor's grounding, the review's withheld readings, the open-items register, the independent reviews before every shipment, the live checks after. What did they catch? What slipped through (the record names each one)?
4. **What was written down?** The build log, the syllabus's decisions, the register, the record of live checks — all public. Could a stranger follow it? Could a student?
5. **Did the course do what it wrote?** The syllabus promised twelve features and twelve lessons; the register says which were built, which were verified live, and which are still open. Read the open ones aloud. A course that ends with an honest list of what it did not finish has followed its own rules.

## 6 · The kit, handed over

Everything on `/kit`, and in the repository's `kit/` folder: the student quickstart, the decision journal, the Starter System rules card, the reading list, the costs-and-tax walkthrough, the scam-spotting notes, and this page. Print the rules card and the journal template; keep the links to the rest. The toolkit — your paper account, the signals, the screen, the calculator, the tutor, the review, the worksheet — stays up behind the door for as long as the tutor says in class, and every line of it is in the open repository for anyone who wants to run their own.

## 7 · The Monday routine, after the course

Five minutes, as from week 7: open your page; read the stop line first; read the rule's state; read the report's sentence; write the Monday line in the journal. Once a week, the review page. Once a year, the costs-and-tax page against the Finance Act of that year. The course ends; the routine does not — that was the point.

*Education, not advice. Nothing in this kit is a recommendation to buy or sell anything; the habits are the point.*
