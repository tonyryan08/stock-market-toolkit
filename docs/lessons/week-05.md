# Lesson 5 — The Graham Checklist (and the evening we argue with it)

**90 minutes · Phase 2, week 2 of 3 · Toolkit feature: the Graham screen `/screen` + the checklist card `/card`**

*Methodology: the seven defensive-investor tests from* The Intelligent Investor *ch. 14 (course copy p. 347; Zweig's commentary p. 367), as Graham revised them in 1971–72; margin of safety from ch. 20 (p. 512). Chapter numbers hold in every edition.*

## By the end, students can

1. State margin of safety in one sentence, and show how each of the seven tests is a version of it.
2. Read the checklist card: what each test asks, and which four rules are adapted (and why).
3. Run the screen, read a verdict row, and explain a failure in plain words.
4. Argue both sides: is a famous company's failure a fault in the company, or in a test written in 1972?

## Materials

- The live screen at `/screen`. Pre-checked the evening before (tutor notes).
- **Printed checklist cards**, one per student: print `/card`. This is the kit item, and it prints from the same rules the screen runs.
- Journals, plus a blank slip per student for the prediction exercise.
- TII ch. 14 and ch. 20 bookmarked. Week 4's flip chart of the four numbers stays on display.

## Running order

**0:00 — Wall of Resulting + harvest (10 min).**
Nominate, vote, pin. Then the chapter 8 reading: where did they spot Mr. Market this week? (Expect notification screenshots. Pin the best one beside the Wall.) Then collect one books-page surprise per table.

**0:10 — Margin of safety, in full (10 min).**
Last week it was one sentence. Tonight it gets the full idea (ch. 20, p. 512). The engineer's version works on any room: a bridge rated for 30 tonnes carries 10-tonne lorries. You never know the true load, so you build the gap in. In investing, the gap is between what you pay and what the business is worth. The gap is there because you will sometimes be wrong. Write on the board: **every test on tonight's card is a different way of building in that gap.**

**0:20 — Walk the card (15 min).**
Hand out the printed cards. Go through the seven tests at kitchen-table pace, using the italic "why" line on each:
- Size: small firms break in bad years.
- Strength: can it pay the bills, and is the mortgage smaller than the cushion?
- Stability: has it never lost money?
- Dividends: has it handed over real cash for a decade?
- Growth: modest growth that has actually happened.
- P/E: don't pay many years of profit up front.
- P/B: something solid behind the price.

Then the four **adapted** stamps, taught as a lesson in their own right:
- Graham asked for 10 years of earnings; free data holds 5.
- He asked for 20 years of dividends; our record holds about 15, so we ask for 10.
- His $100m size floor was in 1971 dollars; ×~8 for inflation gives $800m.
- His growth test was +⅓ over 10 years; we pro-rate that to our shorter window, which comes to +9%. (Strictly, his three-year averages sit seven years apart, so a stricter reading would be +13%. We chose 9% and say so on the card.)

*Honest data use means saying out loud where your data runs short.* Compare a tip-seller's spreadsheet, which never tells you where its numbers ran out.

**0:35 — Predict first (5 min).**
Before anyone sees the screen: each student writes on a slip which **three** companies from the universe will pass all seven. Name, date, sealed. This is a decision-journal entry made *before* the outcome. Process first, then the reveal.

**0:40 — Run the screen (15 min).**
Project `/screen`. Read the tally boxes, then the survivor rows, then the near misses. Open the slips: how many predictions survived? **Expect very few survivors.** That's the design: Graham wrote these tests to *reject*, and a universe of famous mega-caps is exactly where they bite hardest. Graham's own result is the proof (p. 354): applied to the 30 Dow stocks at the end of 1970, **only five passed** — American Can, AT&T, Anaconda, Swift and Woolworth. Then tell them what happened next: Anaconda's Chilean mines were expropriated within the year; Woolworth declined for decades. Passing the screen earns a reading, not safety. Hover a ✗ to show its numbers. Every mark can be explained from the books page, and that's the standard. Read the *Security Analysis* epigraph again: *"Many shall be restored that now are fallen, and many shall fall that now are in honor."*

**0:55 — Argue in pairs (20 min).**
Each pair gets one survivor (or near miss) and one famous failure. If nothing survives and nothing nearly does, take the highest scorers instead (on the first run, Nvidia at 5/7) — the eight-company table in `week-05-reading-the-screen.md` is the ready-made material. Use a household name if possible: a tech giant, a carmaker, an aircraft maker, a bank. Three questions to answer:
1. *Which tests did it fail, in plain words?* (Read the numbers off the hover detail or the books page.)
2. *Is the test right about this company, or out of date?* Two modern frictions to hand them:
   - **Buybacks** shrink book equity, so P/B balloons.
   - **Intangibles** (brands, software, patents) barely appear on the balance sheet at all.

   Banks and other financial companies show "–" on test 2 and are scored out of six. Graham's financial-strength test is written "for industrial companies"; for financials he applied only the price tests (p. 361). The "–" is not a fail, and it doesn't count against them. Zweig concedes the intangibles point himself (p. 374): brands, patents and goodwill are excluded from book value, so "most companies today are priced at higher price-to-book multiples than in Graham's day". The objection comes from inside the book.
3. *Does failing make it a bad investment, or just not a **defensive** one?*

Report back: one sentence per pair. The course takes no side. The skill is arguing from the books rather than from the headlines.

**1:15 — What a screen can't tell you (10 min).**
Four things no screen can show: whether management can be trusted; whether the business has a moat; what happens next; and *why* any number is what it is. The screen narrows the list. Reading decides. Zweig's rule for the reading (p. 375): go to the company's own reports and "read at least five years' worth" — which is exactly what the books page puts in front of them. And his blunt corollary, quoted straight: if you're not willing to do that, "you are too defensive to be buying individual stocks at all. Get yourself out of the stock-picking business and into an index fund, where you belong." His practical compromise (p. 367): keep 90% in an index fund and pick stocks with the other 10%. That is not advice from this course; it is what the book says, and it belongs in the room. Next week brings narrative ("explain this stock"), and with it the danger of stories that outrun the numbers.

**1:25 — Homework + close (5 min).**
1. Read TII **ch. 14**: Graham's text plus Zweig's commentary (p. 347 and p. 367).
2. Journal a **pre-mortem**. Pick one survivor or near miss and answer: *"It's three years from now and this turned out badly. What most likely happened?"* Before writing it, read that company's five-year table on the books page — Zweig's "five years' worth".
3. Keep the card. It's page two of the takeaway kit.

Close with the sentence.

## Tutor notes

- **Pre-check the evening before:** open `/screen` and note the survivor count. Then hover one ✗ on a famous failure and confirm its number against that company's `/fundamentals/` page (the week-4 reconciliation habit).
- **If zero companies survive**, that *is* the lesson. Two readings are both defensible: the market is expensive, or the tests are dated. Put both on the board; don't pick one. The first live run (12–13 Oct 2026) returned exactly zero over 31 companies (the universe is now 38, after the value sweep of 14–15 Oct) — the reading of that result, point by point with Graham's pages, is in `week-05-reading-the-screen.md`, including the eight companies that give the pairs exercise its material.
- **"So should I buy the survivors?"** It will come. The scripted answer: "A survivor has earned a *reading*, not a purchase. Week 6 shows why a story about it isn't enough, and week 8 shows how much you'd ever put in." Park it.
- **Financial companies (JPM, BAC, C, WFC, V) show "–" on test 2 and score out of six.** That's Graham's own rule, not a gap: his financial-strength test is for industrial companies; for financials he applied only the price tests (p. 361). A bank that passes its six is a survivor. (Utilities have separate rules too — size by total assets, debt ≤ 2× equity — but there are none in the universe.)
- **Negative book value shows as a fail on test 7, with "neg. book" on the books page.** McDonald's, Home Depot, Starbucks and Boeing all report negative equity — buybacks or losses have used up the shareholders' funds. Good argument material for the pairs: is that a weakness, or an accounting artefact of returning cash?
- **Exit check (syllabus):** the screen returns from cache in under ~2s (sandbox: ~7–65 ms over 5 companies; expect well under a second for 38). Verdicts must reconcile with a hand check: `npm test` holds 47 screen cases (27 defensive, 16 enterprising, 4 on the dividend gap rule), every one checkable on paper — the count grows as the weeks ship; `npm test` prints the current one. Independently reviewed against the source text on 12 Oct 2026 — `docs/reviews/week-05-independent-review.md`.
