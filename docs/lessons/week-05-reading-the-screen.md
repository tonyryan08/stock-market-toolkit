# Reading the screen — teaching points from the first live runs

**Tutor notes · Phase 2 · a companion to lesson 5 (and the grounding for lesson 6)**

*What this is.* The Graham screen ran for the first time over real books on 12 October 2026 and over the complete 31-company universe on 13 October. These are the teaching points that came out of reading those results — each one tied to the page of* The Intelligent Investor *(course copy, 2003 revised edition) it rests on. The numbers below are a dated snapshot: the pool widens after the value sweep, and the screen's footer row shows the live counts. Keep this file as the record of the first result; Graham's own first result (p. 354) was a dated snapshot too.*

## The result

Universe 31, all with books. **Survivors 0 · near misses 0 · fail two or more 31.** The closest thing to a survivor was Nvidia at 5/7, failing only the two price tests.

## 1. Zero is the screen working

Graham says in so many words that his tests reject the famous growth companies: they are "exclusive in the opposite direction" (p. 349), shutting out the companies "whose shares sell at especially high multipliers" (p. 347). This universe is the 31 most famous companies in the world, chosen for two reasons that have nothing to do with value — students know them (Apple in Cork, Pfizer in Ringaskiddy), and the free data plan serves them. Those are the same reason: data providers whitelist what people search for, and people search for fame.

So "were we fishing in the wrong pool?" — yes, and Graham told us we would be. His own 1970 screen passed five of the thirty Dow stocks (p. 354): American Can, AT&T, Anaconda, Swift, Woolworth — the unglamorous. Two of the five went badly within years. A defensive screen run over the glamour end of the market and returning zero is not a broken screen. It is the screen doing the job it was written for.

*In class:* the 0:40 reveal. Put the prediction slips beside a zero and let that sit.

## 2. Two readings, both on the board — and which way the data leans

The lesson plan's rule stands: if nothing survives, write both readings up — *the market is expensive* and *the tests are dated* — and don't pick one for them. But the room can be shown which way the evidence leans. The "dated tests" argument is about book value: brands, patents and software don't appear on the balance sheet, so P/B is high everywhere (Zweig concedes this himself, p. 374). That explains the 30 fails on test 7. It explains nothing about test 6: not one of these 31 trades below 15 times its three-year average earnings. Graham's own looser rule — an earnings yield at least matching the AA bond yield (p. 350; Zweig's footnote works it to a maximum P/E of 21.7 and a target of about 17 for 2003) — would still reject most of the 31 at today's yields. Zweig found the same in 2003: only about 120 of the S&P 500 passed the 2:1 current-ratio test alone (p. 369).

*In class:* let them do Graham's bond-yield arithmetic themselves with today's AA yield. That is Graham arguing with Graham, and p. 349 says "the reader will have to decide this important question for himself."

## 3. The strength inversion

Who passed test 2, the financial-strength test? Nvidia, Alphabet, Meta, AMD, Tesla — cash-rich technology companies. Who failed it? Coca-Cola, Pepsi, Johnson & Johnson, Walmart, Costco, Apple — the dividend aristocrats. Graham's test, written "for industrial companies" (p. 348), now rewards the growth companies and punishes the steady ones, because the modern consumer giant runs on thin working capital by design: retailers are paid before they pay suppliers, and buybacks are funded with long-term debt.

*In class:* the pairs question — is Apple's 0.9 current ratio weakness, or efficiency? Both answers are defensible from the books. That is the point.

## 4. Eight companies, eight meanings of ✗

A screen reports that a test failed; it cannot report *why*. The first live table happened to contain every kind of why, which is what lesson 6 ("explain this stock") is built on:

| Company | What the ✗ means | Graham |
|---|---|---|
| **Nvidia** 5/7 | Passes every business test, fails only on price. Not a bad company — a price that demands growth a defensive investor doesn't pay for. | p. 349 |
| **Alphabet, Meta** | Fail dividends because the policy began in 2024. A ten-year record is impossible for a two-year payer: *a test that cannot apply yet*, not weakness. | p. 348 |
| **Bank of America** 4/6 | The only company to pass book value — banks trade near book, which is why Graham applied only the price tests to financials. Fails growth. | p. 361 |
| **Delta, Nike** | The only P/E passes — "cheap for a reason." Delta's earnings are cyclical and its dividend was suspended in 2020; Nike's earnings are falling. The value-trap conversation. | p. 165 (Chrysler) |
| **Coca-Cola, J&J** | Fail strength on thin working capital. Weak, or efficient? | p. 348 |
| **Exxon** | Fails growth because the base years (2022–23) were a windfall. Cyclical earnings are exactly why Graham averages three years. | p. 349 |
| **Boeing, Intel** | 1/7 — fail everything but size. Size is the one test a mega-cap cannot fail. | p. 348 |

*In class:* assign one row per pair at 0:55. The report-back sentence is "our company failed test N because…", read off the books page, not the headlines.

## 5. Nobody is cheap on both price measures

Delta and Nike are cheap against earnings; Bank of America is cheap against assets; nobody is cheap against both. Graham's two price tests measure different things — earnings power and asset backing — and the 22.5 product (p. 349) is his compromise for a company that is reasonable on both. A universe with no overlap at all is the sharpest single statement of "expensive" the screen can make.

*Addendum, 17 Oct 2026.* With the value tier in (universe 38), two companies pass both price tests: AT&T and Verizon. Both fail on the business side instead — AT&T on strength and stability (a 2022 loss), Verizon on strength and growth — so the table now holds three 5/7s with disjoint failures: Nvidia fails only on price; AT&T and Verizon fail only on quality. That is Graham's trade-off in one screen, and Wells Fargo became the project's first near miss (5/6, failing only P/E). The statement above stands for the famous 31; the pool was widened precisely to change it.

## 6. The dividend column is a history book

Nine fails on test 4, every one a fact: Alphabet and Meta began paying in 2024; Amazon, Tesla, Netflix, Adobe and AMD have never paid; Disney suspended 2020–23; Delta suspended in 2020. Zweig's table (p. 372) lists companies that have paid every year since the 1800s — Bank of America since 1903 — and the screen found the data agreed. A ten-year record is a statement about the past, not a judgment about the future; it is on the card because Graham wanted evidence of real cash handed over, year after year (the card's own words, not his).

## 7. The stability column is the Boeing lesson

Four fails on test 3, all real loss years: Amazon 2022, GE 2021–22, Boeing every year since 2019, Intel 2024. "Has it never lost money?" is the simplest question on the card and the one most students have never asked of a famous company.

## 8. Verify before you interpret (the method, applied to itself)

The first screen, on 12 October, showed 21 companies with books and ten "blocked." That zero would have been a false result: the gap turned out to be the toolkit hitting a platform limit, not the companies failing. Before anyone interpreted the zero, two checks were made — the dividend record's order (Coca-Cola, J&J and Pfizer showing ✓ in column 4 proved the record was stored the right way round) and the missing ten (fixed, refetched, screened). Only then was the zero called real. One more thing worth telling the room: a prediction was written down that Exxon would pass both price tests on the full data. It didn't. The prediction stays on the record, graded by the data — which is what the decision journal asks of them.

*In class:* this is the week-4 reconciliation habit at scale. A result is only as good as the record behind it; the toolkit's diary (`fetch_log`) is where the record lives.

## 9. Two investors — effort, not risk appetite

Graham's distinction is not about nerve. The defensive investor wants "freedom from effort, annoyance, and the need for making frequent decisions"; "the determining trait of the enterprising investor is his willingness to devote time and care" (p. 6). His enterprising criteria (pp. 385–386) show what the extra effort buys: a cheapness filter first (nine times earnings or less), then five tests *relaxed* from the defensive set — current ratio 1.5, debt up to 110% of working capital, no deficit in five years, "some current dividend", latest earnings above four years earlier — and **no size floor**: "small companies may afford enough safety if bought carefully and on a group basis." One test is stricter: price under 120% of *net tangible* assets. The enterprising investor is a bargain-hunter willing to read small, dull companies the market has walked past. His 1970 list (Table 15-1, p. 387) is Aberdeen Mfg., Allied Mills and Big Bear Stores.

Two consequences for the course. Small companies fail the defensive screen's size test *by design* — the two screens see different pools, which is the cleanest way to show what "willing to engage" buys. And Graham's conditions for small companies are the brief for the paper portfolio in weeks 8–9: "on a group basis" (p. 386), and bargains pay "if you can find enough of them to make a diversified group, and if you don't lose patience" (p. 392). Zweig adds the house rules: practise for a year on paper before real money (pp. 396–397), and cap stock-picking at 10% of the portfolio with the rest in an index fund (p. 397) — which is what this course is.

## 10. Affordable is not the same as cheap

A €10,000 pot makes share price matter — a €2,000 position in a $900 share is two shares and no granularity — but that is a sizing problem for week 8, not a valuation signal. Graham on low-priced shares: "this fact is not truly important" (p. 385). The value pool is weighted to sub-$100 names for the sizing reason; the screens never look at the share price itself.
