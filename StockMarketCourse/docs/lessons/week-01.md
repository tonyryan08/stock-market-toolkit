# Lesson 1 — What Is a Market? (and why we grade process, not outcomes)

**90 minutes · Phase 1, week 1 · Toolkit feature: the live site + `GET /api/hello`**

## By the end, students can

1. Explain in their own words what a share is, what an exchange does, and where a price comes from.
2. Tell the difference between a good decision and a good outcome — and name "resulting" when they see it.
3. Complete their first decision-journal entry (on an everyday decision, not a trade).

## Materials

- Printed decision-journal templates (`kit/decision-journal.md`), one per student plus spares.
- Three chocolate bars (identical) and a bag of small sweets — the auction lots.
- A coin. A flip chart or whiteboard.
- The course site URL written large. Students need their phones for the reveal.

## Running order

**0:00 — Welcome and the promise (10 min).**
Who you are, why this course exists. Make the three promises out loud and write them up: *paper portfolios only; no tips, ever; everything we build is open.* Then the sentence the whole course hangs on: **"In this room we grade decisions, not outcomes."** Tell them it won't make sense yet; it will by 8pm.

**0:10 — What is a share? What is an exchange? (15 min).**
Cocktail-napkin level, no jargon wall: a share is a slice of a real business; you're buying it from another person, not from the company (secondary market); an exchange is just the room — now a computer — where buyers and sellers find each other; **a price is nothing more than the last deal two strangers agreed to.** Take questions but park anything about "which shares are good" — visibly, on a "Parked" list. It builds trust that parked things return.

**0:25 — The sweet auction (15 min).**
Auction the three chocolate bars one at a time, open outcry, paid in the small sweets you dealt out unevenly on purpose. Record each hammer price on the flip chart. Then the debrief, which is the actual lesson: the bars were identical — why did the prices differ? Draw out: who had more sweets (budget), who wanted it more (sentiment), who saw others bidding (momentum!), how the *last* price became "the price" in everyone's head. You have just taught price formation, order flow, and anchoring without naming any of them. Name them now, lightly.

**0:40 — Luck vs skill: the coin-flip champion (15 min).**
Everyone stands. Each round, call heads or tails *before* the flip; wrong guesses sit. Crown the last person standing "the room's best coin predictor" and let that sit for a beat — then ask if anyone would pay them for coin tips. Introduce **resulting**: judging a decision by its outcome. A good outcome from a bad process is luck; you cannot rerun life, so we grade the part we control. Real-world hook: ask for a show of hands who knows someone who "made a killing" on some share or coin — was that skill? How would we ever know? (Answer: process, written down, in advance.)

**0:55 — The decision journal (15 min).**
Hand out the template and walk it field by field. Then everyone completes a practice entry on an everyday decision with genuine uncertainty — the route home tonight, whether to fix or float the mortgage, whether to buy the bigger bag of coal. The point: *thesis before, review after, rules in between.* Every paper trade in this course requires an entry **before** execution — no entry, no trade. That rule is never waived.

**1:10 — The toolkit reveal (15 min).**
Phones out; everyone opens the site. Walk the twelve-week ledger on screen: week 1 is stamped shipped, eleven rows to go. Then the punchline — tap `GET /api/hello` and show them the entire toolkit today is one page and one route. It grows a capability a week, in public, and they can watch the source code the whole way. This is deliberate: the course trusts them with how the sausage is made, because opacity is where the fee-takers and the scammers live. (First scam-literacy seed, planted lightly — it flowers in weeks 6 and 11.)

**1:25 — Homework and close (5 min).**
Two items: (1) bring one financial headline or social-media post that smells like *luck dressed up as skill* — we'll open week 2 with the best ones; (2) keep the journal template somewhere they'll see it. Close by repeating the sentence: decisions, not outcomes.

## Tutor notes

- **"Just tell us one good share."** It will come, probably tonight. The scripted answer, warm but total: *"That's the one thing I'll never do — and by week 6 you'll be able to explain why anyone who does it for free is selling something."* Park it; it pays off in the scam-literacy weeks.
- **"Will we make money from this course?"** Honest answer: the course is designed to stop them *losing* money to bad process, high costs, and scams — which, for most retail investors, is where the real money is.
- **Mixed rooms.** Some students will arrive with trading-app experience and vocabulary. Give them jobs (auctioneer's clerk, coin-flip referee) rather than airtime — keeps the floor level for beginners.
- If the live site misbehaves, the lesson still works: every activity is offline. The reveal moves to week 2 and becomes "why real systems need error handling."

## Exit check (from the syllabus)

Live URL opens on students' phones; `git push` deploys. Both verified before class, not during.
